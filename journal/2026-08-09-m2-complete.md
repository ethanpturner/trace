# 2026-08-09 — Closing M2: the checkpoint, the reviewer's edits, and the interface

Continues `journal/2026-08-09-m2-context-and-seam.md`, which stopped with twelve M2 issues open.
This covers the rest: the runtime and extractor issues that entry listed as open, and then #74
through #77 — the benchmark truth set, the approval checkpoint, the reviewer's mutating actions, and
the command-line surface.

**M2 Context is closed at 20 of 20.** The pipeline now runs end to end for the context slice: a
person can register documents, extract a context, read it with its evidence, edit it, and approve a
baseline, without a provider if they replay a recorded response.

Three decisions came out of it — DEC-038, DEC-039, DEC-040 — and one in-place correction to DEC-032.
Two of the three were forced by an issue asking for something the corpus did not contain, which is
the same pattern M1 and the first half of M2 recorded, arriving from a third direction.

## The pattern, third form: the issue asked for a thing that did not exist

M1's entry recorded documents disagreeing with decisions made after them. The first M2 entry
recorded *issue bodies* disagreeing with decisions made after them. This half found a new variant:
an issue asking for a mechanism nobody had built, phrased as though it were already there.

- **#75 asked rejection to "return the run to extraction through the re-extraction path."** There is
  no such path. `agent-design.md` section 9 lists "request re-extraction" among the reviewer's
  actions and nothing anywhere says what it does to a run.
- **#76 asked a reviewer-added object to carry `source_origin` of `reviewer_edit`.** Only
  `EvidenceReference` and `ContextClaim` had the field.
- **#76 also asked to use `SystemContext.next_version()` "and `ContextClaim.supersedes_id` as DX-16
  directs."** DX-16 is DEC-023, and DEC-023 says `supersedes_id` is explicitly *not* for reviewer
  edits.

The first two became DEC-038 and DEC-039. The third was implemented as the decision directs and
recorded on the issue, with a test asserting no edit ever writes the field.

What makes this tractable is the thing the earlier entries said: issue text is a snapshot and the
decision log is the authority. What is new is that twice the honest answer was not "follow the
decision instead" but "the corpus is silent and something has to be decided now."

## DEC-038 — re-extraction is a new run

The tempting implementation is an edge from `human_context_review` back to `context_extraction`. It
is wrong for reasons that were all already written down:

- `TRANSITIONS` declares a sequence and `successor()` returns one phase. A second successor is the
  one shape it refuses to resolve.
- `agent-design.md` section 27 requires the orchestrator to prevent uncontrolled loops, and
  extraction → validation → review → extraction is the loop. Bounding it means inventing a fourth
  kind of ceiling.
- DEC-017 already makes a rejected review a stopped run. The process that produced the context is
  gone by the time a rejection arrives; what happens next happens in a new invocation either way.
- DEC-031 already allows an assessment several runs.

So re-extraction is the assessment's next `WorkflowRun`, and the `ReviewerDecision` with disposition
`request_more_analysis` is what connects the two. The argument that decided it beyond tidiness: a
single run that visited extraction twice has counters that are the sum of two attempts, and "how
often does a reviewer reject an extracted context" stops being answerable from the ledger. Two runs
answer it by counting.

The entry left one question open — whether the rationale may reach the next run's prompt — and
DEC-040 closed it a day's work later.

## DEC-039 — the field that made an addition visible

`Component`, `Asset`, `Actor`, `DataFlow`, and `TrustBoundary` now carry `source_origin`.

The argument against was real and I nearly took it: section 4.4 says the enum "identifies where
information originated", and it was carried by exactly the two objects that assert something —
`EvidenceReference` and `ContextClaim`. A component is a structural fact, not an assertion. That is
a coherent reading of the corpus.

It stops being coherent at the checkpoint. A reviewer adds a component the extractor missed, and
nothing on the object records that a person created it. `ReviewDisposition` has no member meaning
"added" — section 4.6 names what the system records *about an existing object* — so the addition
would have to be recorded as an approval, which is exactly the row that approving an *extracted*
object also produces. The two would be the same row.

That matters because of what section 2.5 says reviewer edits are *for*: "the evaluation signal that
shows where the workflow was inaccurate." "The reviewer had to add three components the extractor
missed" is the sharpest form of that signal, and it was not computable from the objects.

Cost, recorded honestly in the entry's tradeoffs: required means every construction site states it —
five models, one conversion function, five test modules. And the field answers "who created this",
not "who last touched it", which nothing in the name says.

## DEC-040 — and a correction to work from earlier the same day

DEC-023 says `SystemContext.version` "increments on approval, alongside `approved_at` and
`approved_by`". In #75 I read that loosely: approval stamps whatever revision is current, and a new
version appears when a reviewer's edits change the baseline's membership. I wrote a test asserting
version stays at 1 and a comment saying the bump belonged to the next issue.

The looser reading fails on its own terms. An edit to a claim's text changes no membership list, so
an assessment could be reviewed, edited, and approved with exactly one revision in the store — and
#76's acceptance criterion "leaves the prior revision retrievable" would be satisfied by there being
nothing to retrieve. Two revisions, always, is both simpler to state and the only version that keeps
the generated baseline intact next to the approved one.

So `approve_context` mints the successor. Version 1 is what the extractor produced and is never
approved; version 2 is what a person approved; the difference between them is the reviewer's work
and is readable by diff.

The second half of the entry is the part I would have got wrong by inertia: the successor's
identifier lists are **recomputed from the store**, not copied. That is what makes rejection mean
something. `current-architecture.md` section 5.6 says threat analysis reasons from the approved
baseline; a component the reviewer rejected sitting in that baseline would be reasoned from anyway,
and the rejection would be a row nothing consulted.

The entry also closes DEC-038's open question. DEC-013's trust levels already answer it: the corpus
divides origins into material under review and everything else, and `reviewer_edit` is in the second
group. A reviewer is the operator of the tool, not a document being assessed, so their rationale
goes in the trusted half of the prompt. Putting it inside the source-content fence would tell the
model to treat the operator's instruction as data, which is the fence's distinction inverted.

## #74 — the truth set, and what it must not contain

`expected-context.yaml` is the first expected file authored. The interesting constraint is not the
schema; it is what the file is *allowed to know*.

`forgeflow-scenario.md` sections 5, 7, 8, 10, and 11 enumerate the actors, components, assets, data
flows, and trust boundaries as the scenario's author knows them. The seven input documents describe
the system as its documentation records it. Those are different sets, and grading an extraction
against the former penalises Trace for not knowing what nobody told it — which is the DEC-009 failure
with its sign flipped: rewarding invention instead of punishing silence.

So every entry cites the input document and section it rests on, and a test resolves the citation.
That test earned its place immediately: four of my citations named sections that did not exist, and
one of them ("6. Data Handling") was a heading I had invented while summarising.

The injection tests are the other half. `agent-design.md` section 25 describes fencing, explicit
precedence, and detection, and every one of those is advisory — sentences in a prompt the model
decides what to do with. So each of the six planted instructions gets a test that crafts the
response a *compliant* model would return and asserts the application refuses it: by
`extra="forbid"`, by the absence of a findings field, by the rule that a documented claim cites
evidence, by evidence-reference resolution, and by the validation node. The defence does not rest on
the model behaving.

The sixth instruction — echo a GitHub App private key — has no schema answer, and the honest test is
its precondition: nothing key-shaped is in the prompt, because the package is built from document
metadata and indexed excerpts and there is no field a credential could occupy.

## #75 and #76 — the gate, and what a reviewer does behind it

The gate is not a check that could be skipped; it is the node's return value.
`ContextReviewNode.run` names the `SystemContext` among the objects awaiting a decision whenever
`is_approved` is false, and the orchestrator advances only on an empty `awaiting_review`. The node's
only constructor field is `version`.

One detail worth recording because it was not obvious: the guard reads `approved_at` and
`approved_by` rather than counting `ReviewerDecision` rows. A checkpoint that advanced once every
subject had a decision would advance for a run whose rows were written by something other than
`approve_context` — an evaluation harness replaying recorded decisions, which DEC-012 explicitly
requires to be possible — leaving the revision unapproved and the record unable to say a reviewer
ever saw it. There is a test that writes exactly that row and asserts the gate stays shut.

For #76, the design pressure was all in one place: `data-model.md` section 2.5 forbids *silently*
overwriting generated content, and the load-bearing word is *silently*. So an edit mutates the
object in place and the decision carries the delta — and the property that actually matters is that
the generated value stays recoverable after the object has moved on. There is a test for that
specifically, separate from the ones about the mechanism.

The data-flow correction check is the piece I am happiest with. A correction that would dangle an
endpoint is refused **in the validation node's own words**, because the check calls
`SystemContext.validate_against` — the function that node calls — and compares the problem list
before and after. Two consequences fall out: the two cannot drift into describing the same mistake
differently, and a context that was already inconsistent does not make the next unrelated edit the
reviewer's fault.

## #77 — the interface, and the provider question it exposed

The command surface grew by four. The part that took thought was not the commands.

`trace context extract` needs a model. Running it in a test, or in CI, or for a demo when the
provider is down, needs a model that reaches no provider. `offline-fake` already existed as a
profile with `provider="fake"` — and nothing anywhere turned a profile into a model. Every existing
caller constructed `AnthropicModel` directly, which meant DEC-014's seam was agnostic everywhere
except at the one point where the provider is actually chosen.

`infrastructure/model/factory.py` is that point, and it is one branch on `profile.provider`. Adding
a provider is now an adapter and a branch, and nothing else in the application changes — which is
what "provider-agnostic" is supposed to mean and the only way to check it. The fake is a
first-class provider rather than a test hook, because `current-architecture.md` section 5.1 wants
the CLI usable for repeatable evaluation and for demo recovery, and both need a run that reaches no
provider.

The review file exists because a reviewer with forty claims is not going to pass forty flags. The
property worth having is that the file is a way of *saying* what to do rather than a second
implementation of doing it: both paths call the same functions, and a test exports a file, edits
it, applies it, runs the equivalent flags on a second assessment, and asserts the `ReviewerDecision`
rows come out identical.

One format decision: every action is expressed as a *difference* from what was exported. Exporting
and reapplying without editing writes nothing. A format where the exported state was itself an
instruction would record a decision for every object each time somebody looked at a review.

Two bugs surfaced while wiring the CLI, both of which had been invisible because nothing had asked
the question yet:

- `ExecutionLedger.pause` left the run counters at zero. A paused run is one a person is looking at,
  and "what has this cost so far" is the question they ask — zero was a wrong answer rather than no
  answer. `complete` and `pause` now share one `counters()`.
- `decide_object` wrote `ObjectStatus.APPROVED` onto whatever had a `status` field, and
  `ContextClaim.status` is a `ClaimStatus` with no `approved` among its values. Section 10's
  statuses are epistemic — what kind of assertion the claim is — so approving a claim changes
  nothing on the object and the decision is the whole record. Rejection does have a value.

## Smaller things worth not forgetting

- **`services/context/__init__.py` cannot re-export the pipeline.** `workflow/context_extraction.py`
  imports `input_package`, so a package `__init__` importing `pipeline` — which imports the
  extraction node — makes importing the node import itself. The convenience of a shorter path was
  not worth a circular import, and the docstring says so.
- **`ContextReviewNode` composes `CheckpointNode` rather than subclassing it.** A `slots=True`
  dataclass subclass that redeclares its parent's fields is a trap, and composition reads better
  anyway: the shared node does what both checkpoints share and the wrapper adds the one thing that
  is specific to the first.
- **DEC-032 said "seven commands"; it is thirteen.** Corrected in place, with a note that its own
  revisit trigger — command count or help quality — has not fired but is closer. `context review`
  carries seven flags, which is the first place hand-written `argparse` starts to feel like work.
